BLINDNESS

In our mills, factories and mines, human hands are herded together to dig, to spin and to feed the machines that they have made, and the product of the machine is not theirs. Day after day naked hands without safeguard, without respite must guide the machines under dangerous and unclean conditions. Day after day they must keep firm hold of the little that they grasp of life until they are hardened, brutalized. Still the portent of idle hands grows apace and the hand-to-hand grapple waxes more fierce. O pitiful blindness!

Motherhood is sacred. Everybody agrees on that point-at least in an abstract way. But how long has motherhood really been regarded as sacred?

In Europe it was the custom until quite recently for a woman of all classes, both Catholic and Protestant, to go to church after having arisen from child-birth. But this mother could not enter the church before she had been purified by the priest or minister, she had to kneel outside in the entrance and wait for him to say a prayer over her that petitioned Almighty God to forgive her for her sin of becoming a mother, before she was worthy of entering the house of God.

In most churches this has been done away with. But Ellen Key informs us that the custom is still carried out in many parts of Sweden. It shows that motherhood was not regarded as sacred even at the time of the Reformation. The sacredness of motherhood came in when the women of the upper class began consciously to regulate the number of children that they would bear, and when children in that class consequently became scarce. And as the upper classes in our society create public opinion, motherhood really became sacred.

One is inclined to lay the blame of all our modern folly and ridiculous ways of thinking on modern society, but our modern society is only a child of ancient society where the lot of motherhood was still worse. In India from time immemorial the wcman is nobody, except as the mother of a son. The Hindoo mother that becomes mother only of girls is looked upon as a being cursed by the gods.

The woman in India matures early, and sometimes she is married when she is only a baby. If she becomes a widow while she is a baby she is cursed and doomed to widowhood for life. She often becomes a mother who's still a child of ten or twelve years of age. The little child mother suffers greatly in giving birth to the child. But she is not allowed competent medical aid. No doctor is allowed to see her. She must come in contact with no human male except her husband and father. Bungling, untrained midwives and old, superstitious women are her only assistance.

Western people are not as a rule entertained in the homes of the Hindooas. Mrs. Annie Besant has lived for many years in India, but Annie is mighty careful not to say anything in public of the terror of motherhood in India, as she does not want to offend the Hindooas and the British rulers. However, a friend of Mrs. Besant in California went to India. She believed that she was an incarnation of some great Hindoo prophet, and by that theory she wormed herself into quite a few homes. MARRIAGE AND MISERY

The conventional marriage, nine times out of ten, as contracted among the civilized peoples of Europe, is a deeply immoral relation fraught with the most fatal results for the future of society. It compels those who enter into it, to find themselves involved sooner or later in a conflict between forsworn vows and indestructible love and gives them only two alternatives—vulgar intrigue or ruin. Instead of its being a source for humanity to renew its youth, it is the means of its slow suicide.—Max Nordau.

ELLEN KEY

WOMAN SUFFRAGE

Woman can give suffrage or the ballot no new quality, nor can she receive anything from it that will enhance her own quality. Her development, her freedom, her independence, must come from and through herself. First, by asserting herself as a personality and not as a sex commodity. Second, by refusing the right to anyone over her body; by refusing to bear children, unless she wants them; by refusing to be a servant to God, the State, society, the husband, the family, etc., by making her life simpler, but deeper and richer. That is, by trying to learn the meaning and substance of life in all its complexities, by freeing herself from the fear of public opinion and public condemnation. Only that, and not the ballot, will set woman free, will make her a force hitherto unknown in the world, a force for real love, for peace, for harmony; a force of divine fire, of life giving; a creator of free men and women.
